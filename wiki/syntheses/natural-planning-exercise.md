# The Chapter 3 exercise — plan one project with the five phases

> [!question] Prompt
> What is the hands-on exercise in Chapter 3, and how does it differ from the Chapter 1
> "important exercise"?

## Answer

It's the one-paragraph **"Tente agora mesmo"** drill in **Chapter 3** (p93), under the heading
*"O planejamento natural não é necessariamente normal"*. It has you run one real project through
all five phases of the [[natural-planning-model]], right after the dinner-out example shows the
phases working unconsciously:

> Tente agora mesmo, escolha um projeto novo, emperra ou que possa ser melhorado. Pense no propósito.
> Pense em como seria um resultado bem-sucedido: onde você estaria fisicamente e financeiramente e em
> termos de reputação e tudo mais? Faça um brainstorming sobre os possíveis passos. Organize as
> ideias. Decida as próximas ações. Agora tem mais clareza sobre aonde quer chegar e como o fará? p93

Broken into steps:

0. **Pick a project** — *"escolha um projeto novo, emperra ou que possa ser melhorado."* New,
   stuck, or improvable.
1. **Purpose** — *"Pense no propósito."* The *why*. → phase 1 (propósito e princípios)
2. **Vision** — *"Pense em como seria um resultado bem-sucedido: onde você estaria fisicamente e
   financeiramente e em termos de reputação e tudo mais?"* The *what*. → phase 2 (visualização de
   resultados)
3. **Brainstorm** — *"Faça um brainstorming sobre os possíveis passos."* The *how*. → [[brainstorming]]
4. **Organize** — *"Organize as ideias."* Components, sequences, priorities. → phase 4 (organização)
5. **Next actions** — *"Decida as próximas ações."* → [[next-action]]

The check at the end — *"Agora tem mais clareza sobre aonde quer chegar e como o fará?"* — is the
payoff, same as Chapter 1's: clarity from thinking *about* the project instead of *on* it.

Note that principles are not prompted explicitly — the exercise asks only for purpose — and that it
asks for next action**s** (plural), matching the book's rule that a project is planned enough when
every front that can move has one (p110).

## Supporting detail

**Location.** Raw source: `raw/allen-gtd-book-notes.md`, line 406, Chapter 3, p93.

**Why the heading says "not necessarily normal."** The exercise sits between the dinner example
(the mind plans naturally, p90–92) and the "unnatural" and "reactive" planning models (p94–95). The
point: this natural sequence is how the brain works, yet it is *not* what most organizations
practice — they start with "who has a good idea?" or with action in a crisis. Doing the exercise
deliberately shows the difference.

> [!warning] Five planning phases ≠ five GTD steps
> The "five phases" here are the **vertical** [[natural-planning-model]], not the **horizontal**
> five-step workflow of the [[gtd-method]] ([[capture]] → [[clarify]] → [[organize]] →
> [[reflect]] → [[engage]]). The only overlap is that both end in a [[next-action]]. Like the
> Chapter 1 drill, this exercise does not put anything into a trusted system, review it, or
> execute it. Neither of the book's two early exercises runs the full GTD workflow. Field-by-field
> comparison of the two models: [[horizontal-vs-vertical-planning]].

## Chapter 1 vs Chapter 3 exercise

| | [[gtd-open-loop-exercise\|Ch. 1 "important exercise"]] (p43) | Ch. 3 "tente agora mesmo" (p93) |
|---|---|---|
| Input | the one loop that most occupies your mind | a new, stuck, or improvable project |
| Model exercised | capture + part of clarify (horizontal) | the five natural-planning phases (vertical) |
| Steps | loop → outcome → next action | purpose → vision → brainstorm → organize → next actions |
| Output | one next action | next actions for the project |
| Shared with the other | outcome + next action | vision (≈ outcome) + next actions |

In practice the Chapter 1 drill is the lighter version: its "outcome" is phase 2 and its "next
action" is phase 5. The Chapter 3 exercise adds purpose before the outcome and brainstorm/organize
in between, for projects where outcome + next action alone don't get it off your mind (p111).
