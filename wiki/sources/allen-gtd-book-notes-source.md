## Summary

Reading notes covering David Allen's *Getting Things Done* (GTD), revised edition: the introduction,
Part 1 (the art and the models), Part 2 (practicing stress-free productivity, chs. 4–10), Part 3
(the power of the key principles, chs. 11–13), chapter 14 (GTD and cognitive science), and the
**appendix glossary of GTD terms** (p343).

The book's core argument: stress comes from poorly managed **internal agreements** — [[open-loops|open loops]]
that the mind keeps tracking. The fix is not more willpower but a trusted **external system**:
capture everything, clarify what each item means and what "done" and "doing" look like, organize the
results, reflect on them regularly (anchored by the [[weekly-review|Weekly Review]]), and engage with
confidence. This is the five-step workflow ([[capture]] → [[clarify]] → [[organize]] → [[reflect]] →
[[engage]]) that produces the "[[mind-like-water|mind like water]]" state. Two complementary models
round it out: the [[natural-planning-model|Natural Planning Model]] (purpose/principles → vision →
[[brainstorming]] → organization → [[next-action|next actions]]) for project thinking, and the
[[horizons-of-focus|Horizons of Focus]] for altitude. Chapter 14 grounds the method in cognitive
science: [[distributed-cognition]], Baumeister's work on incomplete tasks, flow theory, and
implementation intentions.

## Key claims

- Anything held only in your head is an **open loop** that consumes attention; capture it into a trusted external system ("Use a mente para tirar coisas da mente", p43). See [[open-loops]].
- The five steps must be **separated in time** — people fail by trying to capture, clarify, and organize simultaneously (p59). See [[gtd-method]].
- "**Qual é a próxima ação?**" is the most important question; deciding the next physical, visible action is what unsticks everything (p66, p292–302). See [[next-action]].
- You can't do a project, only actions related to it (p71). See [[organize]].
- The **Weekly Review** is "a chave mágica da sustentabilidade do processo" (p231). See [[weekly-review]].
- Planning should follow the mind's natural five phases; the "who has a good idea?" approach is backwards (p90–95). See [[natural-planning-model]].
- Science agrees: you don't need to *finish* tasks to relieve mental load — a trusted plan with a next action is enough (Baumeister, p319); pre-deciding action+context ("implementation intentions") makes behavior near-automatic (p323).
